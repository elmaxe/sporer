# Animals: procedural bodies, gaits and herds (the animal lab)

## Question

Habitable worlds get procedurally generated animals, like their plants (`gen/animalForm.ts` grows a body, `gen/animals.ts` gives a planet its species and herds, `surface/SurfaceAnimals.ts` draws and walks them, `animals.html` is the animal lab). Needed:

1. How games generate and animate creatures they have never seen: Spore, No Man's Sky and others.
2. How animals walk at different sizes and gravities: speed, stride length, when they change gait.
3. Which legs move together in a walk and a trot, for four, six and two legs.
4. How body mass relates to leg length (for a mass from the generated body) and how legs thicken with size and gravity.
5. How common animals are by size, how rare hunters are next to their prey, and how big herds and packs are.
6. Coat rules: countershading and patterns.
7. What makes an animal look cute (asked for after the first version, along with rounder bodies and smooth shading instead of flat facets).

Precision: the right trends and roughly the right numbers for gameplay. The look is stylised (low-poly, sizes scaled up so animals read from the UFO's height; see *Game mapping*).

## Sources

A research agent read these in this session (accessed 2026-10-04) and quoted each value from the page. I read the Alexander formula myself from the dinosaur trackway paper below.

**Creature generation in games**

- **Chris Hecker, "My Liner Notes for Spore"**, https://chrishecker.com/My_Liner_Notes_for_Spore:
  - "I chose a blobby implicit surface (sometimes called metaballs) to represent the skin."
  - "We only use spherical metaballs, and we distribute them along the limbs and torso".
  - "We generate bone weights for the vertices based on which body parts generated which metaballs."
- **Hecker et al. 2008, "Real-time Motion Retargeting to Highly Varied User-Created Morphologies", SIGGRAPH**, https://www.chrishecker.com/Real-time_Motion_Retargeting_to_Highly_Varied_User-Created_Morphologies:
  - Each foot has a "duty factor … the fraction of the duration of the overall gait cycle time that the foot is on the ground" and a "step trigger … the offset from the overall gait cycle at which the foot begins its particular cycle".
  - Foot paths are authored "in a normalized space … then applied to each foot, taking into account the leg length."
  - "Animators author a mapping between character movement speed and the gait parameters for groups with 1 ≤ nfeet ≤ 6".
- **"Development of No Man's Sky"** (Wikipedia, citing Duncan's GDC 2015 talk), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Development_of_No_Man%27s_Sky:
  - They "would first hand-create core structures … such as a basic skeleton and skin for a creature – and then allow the algorithm to make randomized changes to that".
  - "Creatures were often generated by mixing and matching random parts from a library, and then adjusting the underlying skeleton so that the creature appeared realistic; a creature with a tiny body could not support a giant head".
- **Rain World (Jakobsson, GDC 2016, quoted by Game Developer)**, https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/crafting-the-complex-chaotic-ecosystem-of-i-rain-world-i-: "I have a bunch of points in space and I connect them at certain distances", with a "paper doll" of parts on top.
- **Coros et al. 2011, "Locomotion Skills for Simulated Quadrupeds"**, https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2011-TOG-quadruped/paper.pdf: "Progress made within a stride is defined by the gait phase, Φ ∈ [0, 1), computed as Φ = t/T". Gait graphs come from video of a dog.

**Gaits**

- **Alexander (1976), as quoted in Mazzetta & Blanco 2001, Acta Palaeontologica Polonica 46(2)**, https://www.app.pan.pl/archive/published/app46/app46-235.pdf (read from the PDF):
  - "animals … (i.e., with equal Froude numbers) require equal values of relative stride length (ratio between the stride length s and the height at the hip joint h)".
  - "Alexander (1976) proposed the following equation for estimating the speeds of dinosaurs from trackways: v = 0.25 g^0.5 s^1.67 h^−1.17".
  - "extant terrestrial mammals change from a walk to a run or trot when the ratio of stride length to hip height (s/h) reaches a value of about 2.0".
  - Solving the formula for s gives s/h = 2.3·(v²/gh)^0.3. Cross-check: Alexander 2006, "Dinosaur biomechanics", https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1634776/, says he "predicted that similar animals running with equal Froude numbers … would have equal relative stride lengths" and that "it is approximately true for bipedal and quadrupedal mammals".
- **Kram, Domingo & Ferris 1997, J Exp Biol 200:821**, https://spot.colorado.edu/~kram/walk-run.pdf:
  - "humans and other bipeds with different leg lengths all choose to switch from a walk to a run at different absolute speeds but at approximately the same Froude number (0.5)".
  - Measured at 1 g: "1.98 m s−1 … Froude number (v2/gL) of 0.45" with "L=0.89 m".
  - At 0.4 g the transition Froude number was 0.56; at 0.2 g, 0.83; at 0.1 g, 1.13.
- **Froude number** (Wikipedia, citing Alexander 1984 and 1989), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Froude_number: animals "typically switch from an amble to a symmetric running gait (e.g., a trot or pace) around a Froude number of 1.0". Asymmetric gaits are preferred "between 2.0 and 3.0".
- **Preferred walking speed** (Wikipedia), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preferred_walking_speed: people walk "between 1.10 m/s and 1.65 m/s", average "1.4 m/s".
- **Heglund & Taylor 1988, J Exp Biol 138:301**, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3193059/: preferred trotting and galloping speeds "scale with about the 0.2 power of body mass"; stride frequencies with "the -0.15 power".
- **Duty factor**: Wikipedia "Gait", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gait: "Duty factors over 50% are considered a 'walk', while those less than 50% are considered a run."

**Footfalls**

- **Miller et al. 2019, PeerJ**, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6380004/fullTextXML:
  - Limb phase is "the percentage of the stride cycle between the touchdown of a hindlimb and its ipsilateral forelimb".
  - "A limb phase value of 50% represents the trot".
  - "Limb phase values of 25 and 75% indicate that all four footfalls are equally spaced in time".
  - At "25%" the walk follows "a lateral-sequence (LS) footfall pattern". Measured walks "cluster around limb phase values of 15 and 65%".
- **Hutchinson & Pringle 2024, PeerJ (hippos)**, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11227274/fullTextXML: a trot is limb phases "between ~0.44 and 0.56". Measured "RH = 0.51 ± SD 0.051, RF = 0.94 ± SD 0.040 and LF = 0.46".
- **Insects**: Wikipedia "Walking", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walking: in a tripod gait "The middle leg of one side swings with the hind and front legs on the contralateral side". **DeAngelis et al. 2019, eLife (Drosophila)**, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6598772/fullTextXML: "all contralateral pairwise relative phases maintain a mean relative phase of a half-cycle (Δϕ ≈ 0.5)".

**Body scaling**

- **Kilbourne & Hoffman 2013, PLoS ONE (44 mammal species)**, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3832634/fullTextXML: fore- and hindlimb length scale with body mass "with slopes of 0.40 and 0.37".
- **Mohamed Thangal & Donelan 2020, PLoS ONE, Table 2**, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6999919/fullTextXML (same data, fitted as y = a·M^b, M in kg): "Hindlimb length (m) 1.63×10⁻¹ … 0.36".
- **Silva 1998, J. Mammalogy**, https://academic.oup.com/jmammal/article/79/1/20/915006: head-and-body length scales with mass by "0.359", close to geometric similarity (0.333).
- **Elastic similarity** (McMahon 1973): bone diameter ∝ length^1.5. The derivation with gravity is my own (see *Game mapping*); no source read this session gives the gravity term.

**Ecology**

- **Damuth 1981, Nature 290:699**, https://complexityexplorer.s3.amazonaws.com/supplemental_materials/6.8+Scaling/damuth1981a.pdf:
  - Data from "307 mammal primary consumers".
  - "log D = -0.75 (log W) + 4.23", D per km², W in g.
  - Densities vary "within about one order of magnitude from the value predicted".
- **Carbone & Gittleman 2002, Science 295:2273**, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11910114/: "10,000 kilograms of prey supports about 90 kilograms of a given species of carnivore, irrespective of body mass". Bhandari et al. 2024, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11460986/fullTextXML, repeats it.
- **Group sizes** (Wikipedia species pages):
  - Lions: "around 15", "up to 30".
  - Wolves: average pack "eight … and 5.5 in Europe".
  - African buffalo: "45 to 150", Cape buffalo herds "tend to exceed a thousand".
  - Blue wildebeest: "groups of a few to thousands".

**Colouration**

- **Countershading** (Wikipedia), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countershading: "Countershading, or Thayer's law, is a method of camouflage in which an animal's coloration is darker on the top or upper side and lighter on the underside of the body."
- **Allen et al. 2012, Am Nat 180:762 (114 ruminants)**, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23149401/: countershading is "perhaps the most common coloration phenotype in the animal kingdom". It is stronger in "open lighting environments … and small body size", and "noncountershaded or reverse-countershaded species were unexpectedly common".
- **Allen et al. 2011, Proc R Soc B (felids)**, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3061134/: "Patterning of any kind was associated with closed environments"; "Cats found in the open were more likely to be plain".
- **Caro & Mallarino 2020, TREE**: "periodic stripes and spots, arise from spatially constrained developmental processes".

**Cuteness**

- **Glocker et al. 2009, PNAS 106(22):9115, "Baby schema modulates the brain reward system in nulliparous women"**, abstract via Europe PMC (PMID 19451625, accessed 2026-10-04): "Ethologist Konrad Lorenz defined the baby schema ("Kindchenschema") as a set of infantile physical features, such as round face and big eyes, that is perceived as cute and motivates caretaking behavior".
- **Golle, Lisibach, Mast & Lobmaier 2013, PLoS ONE 8(3):e58248, "Sweet Puppies and Cute Babies: Perceptual Adaptation to Babyfacedness Transfers across Species"**, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0058248 (read from the page):
  - "The Kindchenschema is characterized by pedomorphic features such as a relatively large head compared to the size of the body, a relatively big cranium compared to the facial bones, large eyes that lie below the horizontal midline of the skull, a soft-elastic surface texture, and round and protruding cheeks."
  - On Glocker et al. 2009's manipulated faces: "A round face, a high forehead, big eyes, a small nose, and a small mouth were defined as "high" Kindchenschema features. They found that cuteness ratings were positively related to the extent of the Kindchenschema."
  - On Lorenz: "cuteness may be represented by a species-unspecific coding mechanism, since infant faces of different species are characterized by the same facial features (e.g. large eyes, big cranium, etc)".
- A search summary also lists "short and thick extremities and plump body shape" in Lorenz's list; I didn't read that in a primary source this session, so it's marked unverified below.

What the numbers show:

- **Walking.** A person's 0.89 m legs walk at 1.48 m/s at Fr 0.25. That is inside the measured 1.10–1.65 m/s range, 6% above its 1.4 average.
- **Alexander's formula.** It gives the trot speed back from the stride within 0.5%. That is the rounding of its exponents.
- **Walk–run switch.** Fr 0.5 for those legs is 2.09 m/s, against Kram et al.'s measured 1.98 m/s (their Fr 0.45).
- **Mass from legs.** Inverting the hind-limb fit gives 1.0, 101.5 and 1000 kg for the hip heights it predicts at 1, 100 and 1000 kg. The 1.5% at 100 kg is the fit's rounding.
- **Hunters.** They make 14% of herds, as `CARNIVORE_SHARE` asks.

Pinned in `tests/animals.test.ts`:
- the walking Froude number, and a walk inside people's measured speeds;
- the stride rule and Alexander's formula, within 5%;
- slower walks in low gravity, and strides the same number of hip heights long at any size;
- the mass fit;
- the gait phases;
- feet on the ground and bodies above it;
- the herds' Damuth and Carbone trends;
- herds moving smoothly and staying near home, as pure functions of time.

## Game mapping

**Bodies** (`gen/animalForm.ts`):
- **Skin.** Spore wraps an implicit skin round spheres along a spine. This game uses its low-poly equivalent: an elliptical ring at each spine node (tail, torso, neck, head), joined into one tube with a dome at each end. Unlike the game's faceted rocks, animals are smooth-shaded (Golle et al.'s "soft-elastic surface texture"): each vertex's normal comes from the surface it lies on (`surface/animalMesh.ts`), and the vertex shader turns normals with the parts it swings, so a walking leg stays lit as it stands. Legs end in round paws, antennae in bobbles.
- **Body plans.** Three hand-made plans, as No Man's Sky does: four legs, six legs (a thorax carrying the legs and an abdomen behind, sprawling legs, antennae) and two legs (a body balanced over the hips by a long tail, optional arms). Proportions are randomised within each plan's ranges and editable in the lab.
- **Rig.** Every vertex carries its rig like Spore's bone weights: its part, its leg's gait phases, its weight along the part, and its joint. The vertex shader (`surface/animalLook.ts`) does the animation from four numbers per animal, so thousands can share one instanced draw per species and level of detail.

**Gait** (`gen/animals.ts` `animalGait`, units read as metres, g = 9.81 × the planet's gravity in g):
- **Speeds.** Walking speed √(0.25·g·h) and trotting speed √(1.0·g·h), with h the hip height. The walk's Fr 0.25 is people's preferred walk (0.22). The trot's Fr 1.0 is where quadrupeds go from an amble to a trot.
- **Strides.** Stride length = 2.3·Fr^0.3·h at each pace.
- **Phase.** An animal's legs move by the stride cycles it has walked (`cycle` = distance / stride), so its feet keep pace with the ground at any speed and size.
- **Gravity.** Low gravity slows the walk as √g. Kram et al.'s transition Froude number rises below about 0.4 g; the game keeps the constant Fr (an open question below).

**Leg swing** (`animalMotion`):
- A leg swings sinusoidally about its hip (or about the vertical, for sprawling insect legs).
- The amplitude A makes a foot sweep back half a stride during the half cycle it moves back: 2·A·r = λ/2, with r the hip height (or the reach to the side).
- **Stylised:** with a sinusoid, the foot matches the ground's speed only on average, not standing still through the stance as a real foot does. Hecker's duty factors and authored foot paths would fix it, at the cost of IK in the shader.

**Footfalls** (now `creatureMotion.ts` `legPhase`, which gives these phases):
- Four legs: the lateral-sequence walk, LH 0, LF 0.25, RH 0.5, RF 0.75 (the "singlefoot", evenly spaced), and the trot in diagonal pairs (LF with RH, RF with LH) half a stride apart. The trot mix blends the two by speed.
- Six legs: alternating tripods, L1 L3 R2 against R1 R3 L2.
- Two legs: half a stride apart; arms swing against the leg on their side.

**Cuteness** (`generateAnimalForm`), each baby-schema feature as a parameter:
- *A relatively large head, a round face, a big cranium compared to the face:* the head is 32–44% of the body length (`headSize`), most of it a round skull a little wider than tall (round cheeks), set up on the neck (a high forehead), with a short snout (`snout` 5–32% of the head).
- *Large eyes below the skull's midline:* eyes are 27–38% of the skull's radius (`eyeSize`), set just below the midline, with a white eyeball, an iris, a dark pupil and a highlight.
- *Small nose and mouth:* the snout is short and narrows to a round nose.
- *Plump body, short thick limbs (unverified, see Sources):* the old generated bodies drew this from the baby schema. The game now draws its species as the creature editor's random creatures (`gen/creature.ts` `speciesDesign`), so their proportions come from `randomCreature`.
- Coats are brighter (saturation 0.45–0.75) and the pattern is drawn per pixel with soft edges (`surface/animalLook.ts`).
- **Stylised**: the schema describes infants' faces; applied here to adult animals' whole bodies, as games do, not a measured rule for animals.

**Mass and legs:**
- `animalMass(h) = (h / 0.163)^(1/0.36)` kg, from Mohamed Thangal & Donelan's hind-limb fit. Hip height stands in for hind-limb length.
- Leg thickness follows elastic similarity with gravity. A leg's buckling load ∝ d⁴/l² must carry M·g with M ∝ d²·l, so d² ∝ g·l³ and d ∝ g^½·l^1.5. A leg's radius as a share of its length therefore grows as √(g·l), relative to a 2 m animal at 1 g (`legGirthFor`, a factor on the creature's leg size clamped to 0.7–1.6).

**Abundance** (`generateHerd`):
- Each herbivore's herd share is ∝ (M / M₀)^−0.75 (Damuth, capped at 8× so the smallest don't swamp the rest) × how lush the ground is (`fertility`, as for plants) × its climate window.
- The hunters together take `CARNIVORE_SHARE` = 0.15 of the herbivores' share. By Carbone & Gittleman, 0.9% of prey biomass is carnivore: a herd of eight 100 kg grazers supports 7 kg of hunter, a pack of three 100 kg hunters per ~40 herds (2.5%). **Stylised** about fivefold so a player meets one.
- A cell holds a herd at the tier's chance (`HERD_CHANCE_PER_TIER`, gameplay).

**Herds:**
- Real grazers' herds are tens to thousands, and predators' groups about 5–15 (wolves 5.5–8, lions ~15).
- The game draws 3–16 grazers and 1–6 hunters per group. **Stylised** smaller, to keep the instanced draws and the per-animal ground samples cheap.

**Young** (`generateHerd`, `HerdPath`):
- Sources (searched 2026-10-08): a 1962 Alaska elk survey (ADF&G, https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/federal_aid/64_elk_bis_batchelor_burris.pdf) counted 98 calves among 452 elk (22%), the calf–cow ratio averaging 26 per cent (23–33). Oregon's ODFW (https://omls.oregon.gov/pipermail/odfw-news/2005-April/000448.html) calls herds with 32–45 calves per 100 cows stable or growing; the Jackson, Wyoming herd's 20-year average is 26 per 100 (https://pinedaleonline.com/news/2007/03/JacksonElkCounted.htm).
- So each herd or pack has 0.2–0.45 young per adult (`YOUNG_PER_ADULT`, hashed per herd), rounded: a lone animal has none, a pair at most one. They come on top of the adults (`HerdData.young`, the last members), so the adults are as they were.
- A young one is its species' own body (the creature editor's design, `speciesBody`) scaled down to 0.5–0.67 (`YOUNG_SCALE`; adults 0.85–1.15). **Stylised**, as Spore's babies: no change of proportions, small enough to read as young from the ship.
- Each keeps beside an adult (its mother, hashed; twins share one), about 0.75 of its species' length from her, starting and stopping with her. In a panic it runs her way, as far, a moment after her.
- It walks by its own size (dynamic similarity, as for species): strides × its size, speeds × √its size. Keeping up with the herd it takes more strides and trots sooner, and its feet stay planted. The adults' own size now sets their strides too.
- It calls higher: pitch ∝ M^−0.4 and M ∝ size³, so × size^−1.2 (2.3× at half size; `sizePitch`).

**Sizes:**
- Head-and-body length 1.2–7 units in three size classes. Real animals from a fox to a bison are 0.6–3 m.
- **Stylised up** (Spore-like) so animals read from the UFO's height next to its 4 units and the game's 6–11 unit trees. The gait still treats a unit as a metre.

**Coats:**
- Countershading on every animal: the back's colour on faces looking up, the belly's (paler, less saturated) on those looking down. This is Thayer's law, "the most common coloration phenotype".
- Plain, striped, spotted or patched, at weights 3 : 2 : 2.5 : 1.5 (gameplay; the felid study ties patterns to closed habitats, which the game doesn't model yet). The pattern is drawn per pixel by the material from the rest-pose position, so it has soft round edges and moves with the body.
- Colours are converted from sRGB to linear light before they become vertex colours, so a coat shows the colour picked.

**Eyes and horns:**
- Hunters' eyes face forward (`eyesForward` 0.65–1) and grazers' sit on the sides (0–0.25).
- Only grazers grow horns (stylised from the familiar pattern; not from a source read this session).

**Where they live:**
- Animals live wherever plants grow (`growsPlants`: a habitable tier with starlight). A world terraformed into a tier (`terraform`, e.g. in the planet lab) gains them.
- More species the higher the tier: one grazer at T1; two grazers and a hunter at T2; three grazers and two hunters at T3 (gameplay).

**Timekeeping:**
- Herds follow waypoints on a slot grid hashed from the herd and the slot number, so the clock alone sets where they are, as the game's other seeded events do.
- Each walk eases in and out (smoothstep), so it starts and stops gently.
- A herd turns towards its next waypoint over 3 s before setting off.
- Grazers graze in bouts while resting.

## Open questions

- **Plump bodies and short thick limbs.** Seen only in a search summary of Lorenz's list, not in a source read this session; the round face, big cranium, large low eyes, small nose and soft texture are from the sources above.

- **Gravity and the walk–run switch.** Kram et al. found the transition Froude number rising below ~0.4 g (0.83 at 0.2 g, 1.13 at 0.1 g). The game keeps Fr constant, so animals on very light worlds trot sooner than they would.
- **Quadruped walking Froude number.** No source read gives the preferred walking Froude number of quadrupeds; people's 0.22 stands in. Heglund & Taylor's preferred trot speed ∝ M^0.2 was not checked against the game's √(g·h) (the coefficient wasn't read).
- **Hip height and mass.** No exponent for hip height against mass was found; hind-limb length stands in for hip height in `animalMass`.
- **Elastic similarity.** McMahon's own paper wasn't read this session. The d ∝ l^1.5 rule and the gravity term are a derivation, not a quoted value.
- **Herd sizes and hunters.** Both are stylised as described above. Real herds are far bigger.
- **Feet in the stance.** With the sinusoidal swing, feet slide a little through the stance; see *Leg swing*.
