# Animals panicking and calling (issue #156)

## Question

Animals should react to the UFO: bolt when it flies close (issue #156, "Animals panic when ship flies close"), and be heard: procedural calls, herds talking to each other, sounds for what they do (grazing, spotting the ship, panicking, being grabbed or shot). Needed:

1. **Panic**: how close a threat gets before animals look up and before they flee, how far and how fast they run, and what they do after.
2. **Voices**: what pitch an animal of a given size calls at, what its vocal tract does to the sound (formants), how long a call lasts.
3. **Meaning**: how alarm, distress, hostile and friendly calls differ, and what makes a scream (an animal carried off by the beam screams all the way up).

Code: `gen/panic.ts` (a herd's panic, laid over its clock-driven walk), `gen/animalVoice.ts` (a species' voice and the shape of each kind of call), `gen/animalTalk.ts` (when herds talk), `audio/animalSynth.ts` (the Web Audio synthesiser), `surface/SurfaceAnimals.ts` (triggers panics, events), `surface/AnimalSounds.ts` (schedules, spatialises and plays the calls).

Precision: the right trends (bigger animals lower and slower, alarms high, growls low and harsh, gallops at the same Froude number) and plausible numbers. Distances are stylised to the scene (see *Game mapping*).

## Sources

Accessed 2026-10-08.

- **Fletcher, N. H. (2004), "A simple frequency-scaling rule for animal communication", J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 115(5), 2334–2338**, https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/music/people/publications/Fletcher2004.pdf (PDF read in full):
  - Abstract: "the optimal communication frequency is inversely proportional to about the 0.4 power of the animal's body mass … For animals of a given class, for example mammals, the maximum communication distance varies about as the 0.6 power of the animal's mass."
  - Fig. 3 (dominant frequency against body mass), boxes read off the plot: cats ~4 kg at ~500–1000 Hz, dogs ~20 kg at ~200–600 Hz, humans ~70 kg at ~100–250 Hz, elephants ~4000 kg at ~20–50 Hz; horses sit well above the line.
  - Atmospheric absorption "about 0.5 dB/100m at 1 kHz" (α = 3.6×10⁻⁸ m⁻¹ Hz⁻¹·⁵, γ = α f^1.5).
  - "the formants … have a frequency centroid at about 10 times the fundamental frequency in most animals."
- **Moore, R. K. & Mitchinson, B. (2017), "A Biomimetic Vocalisation System for MiRo"**, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.05472 (PDF read, section 2): a generic land mammal's voice from its body mass M (kg):
  - Lung capacity C = 53.5·M^1.06 ml (eq. 1); breathing rate B = 0.84·M^−0.26 Hz (eq. 2); "breathing, and hence vocalisation, only uses a proportion of the air in the lungs (42%)", flow Q = 0.32·C·B (eq. 4).
  - "the mean fundamental frequency F (in kHz) of the vocal fold vibration for animals ranging in size from mice to elephants … F = M^−0.4" (eq. 5, citing Fletcher).
  - Formants of a uniform tube "closed at the vocal folds and open/closed at the mouth": Rn = (2n − (m + 1))·c / 4L, m the mouth's closure, 0 open to 1 closed (eq. 6).
  - "vocal tract length is correlated with body size: L = 3.15 + (11.53 × log M)" cm (eq. 7).
  - "To a first approximation, the signal generated by the glottis may be modelled as a 'sawtooth' waveform"; breathy sounds by "the injection of an appropriate level of random noise"; MiRo's voice has a "fundamental frequency (760 Hz)" and a "vocal tract length (6.6 cm)".
- **Reby & McComb (2003) on red deer roars**, via the search result for Iberian red deer (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3381621/figure/fig05 and the Reby et al. abstracts the search summarised): "an estimated minimum formant dispersion of 228.15 Hz corresponded to an estimated maximum vocal tract length of 767 mm during roars", modelled as "a uniform quarter wavelength resonator". The paper's full text was behind a captcha; the two numbers are from the search summary.
- **Ghazanfar et al. (2007), "Vocal-Tract Resonances as Indexical Cues in Rhesus Monkeys"**, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2361420/ (read through WebFetch): synthetic coos with vocal tracts of "10 cm (large) and 5.5 cm (small)", "within the species-typical range for rhesus monkeys"; the monkeys shown weighed 9.0 and 5.9 kg.
- **Morton, E. S. (1977), "On the occurrence and significance of motivation-structural rules in some bird and mammal sounds", American Naturalist 111, 855–869** (metadata confirmed on Crossref; the abstract as quoted by the search of the Smithsonian repository): "Birds and mammals use harsh, relatively low-frequency sounds when hostile and higher-frequency, more pure tonelike sounds when frightened, appeasing, or approaching in a friendly manner."
- **Arnal, L. H., Flinker, A., Kleinschmidt, A., Giraud, A.-L. & Poeppel, D. (2015), "Human screams occupy a privileged niche in the communication soundscape", Current Biology 25(15), 2051–2056**, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4562283 (read through WebFetch): "human screams cluster within restricted portion of the acoustic space (between ∼30–150 Hz modulation rates)", heard as roughness, while "slow temporal modulations below 20 Hz … encode linguistic meaning"; roughness is in natural and artificial alarms too and speeds their detection.
- **Wikipedia, "Froude number"** (raw wikitext read), citing Alexander (1984), "The gaits of bipedal and quadrupedal animals", Int. J. Robotics Research 3(2): animals "switch from an amble to a symmetric running gait (e.g., a trot or pace) around a Froude number of 1.0. A preference for asymmetric gaits (e.g., a canter, transverse gallop, rotary gallop, bound, or pronk) was observed at Froude numbers between 2.0 and 3.0."
- **Wikipedia, "Flight zone"** (raw wikitext read):
  - The alert distance is "the distance, by definition greater, within which the animal changes its behaviour in a manner enabling it to better observe the stimulus, as by raising the head in an alert posture, but does not necessarily flee unless the stimulus is also within the escape distance" (citing Ruddock & Whitfield 2007).
  - "When the flight zone of a group of bulls was invaded by a mechanical trolley, the bulls moved away and maintained a constant distance between themselves and the trolley" (Kilgour 1971); "animals sometimes maintain a flight zone around unfamiliar inanimate objects."
  - Wild animals "can have very large flight zones"; "larger species are more tolerant of humans" (Blumstein 2015, 212 species), against Blumstein 2006's birds, where "larger species initiat[e] flight at greater distances" (search result summary). Contradictory, so the flight distance here doesn't depend on size.
- **Frid, Dall's sheep and helicopter overflights (Yukon reports, 1997–1999)**, via the search summary only (the PDFs didn't download): "Sheep always had a high escape probability when the helicopter was within 3 km"; guidelines recommend flying more than 3.5 km from sheep ranges.

## Measurements

The voice rules against their reference cases (inline, rerun with `node -e`):

```js
const f = (m) => 1000 * m ** -0.4;                    // Hz, Moore & Mitchinson eq. 5
const L = (m) => 3.15 + 11.53 * Math.log10(m);        // cm, eq. 7
```

| Case | Mass | F = M^−0.4 kHz | Fletcher Fig. 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cat | 4 kg | 574 Hz | ~500–1000 Hz |
| Dog | 20 kg | 302 Hz | ~200–600 Hz |
| Human | 70 kg | 183 Hz | ~100–250 Hz |
| Elephant | 4000 kg | 36 Hz | ~20–50 Hz |

All four fall within their boxes (pinned in `tests/animalPanic.test.ts`).

| Case | Measured | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Red deer roar: c = 2·L·ΔF | 767 mm, 228.15 Hz | c = 350.0 m/s, the speed of sound in warm, moist air: the quarter-wave tube holds |
| MiRo, built from these equations | 760 Hz and 6.6 cm | both give M = 1.99 kg: consistent |
| Rhesus, 9 kg | ~10 cm (Ghazanfar) | 14.2 cm |
| Rhesus, 5.9 kg | ~5.5 cm | 12.0 cm |
| Human, 70 kg | ~17 cm (unverified, from memory) | 24.4 cm |

The tract-length fit runs long for primates (see *Open questions*). Formant spacing in the game uses c = 350 m/s with the formula's L.

Longest utterance on one breath: 42% of C at Q = 0.32·C·B lasts 0.42 / (0.32 × 0.84) = 1.56·M^0.26 s.

**The generated voices** (60 species from 12 tier-3 planets, `animalVoice`): masses from 3 kg (a 1.3-unit grazer) to 11.8 t (a 5.8-unit strider). The fundamentals run from 650 Hz down to the 70 Hz floor (7 of 60 sit on it). Tracts run 9–46 cm, F1 190–960 Hz. Syllables last 0.27–1.2 s (capped) and talk slots 5–14 s.

**The synthesiser, rendered offline** (an `OfflineAudioContext` in the browser, every kind of call by 8 species at gain 1; peak and the dominant frequency from a DFT 30% in). After normalising the rasp's modulation so it never lifts the source:

| Kind | Peak (range over species) | Intended loudness |
|---|---|---|
| contact | 0.43–0.66 | 0.7 |
| answer | 0.34–0.59 | 0.6 |
| grunt | 0.14–0.38 | 0.45 |
| alert | 0.24–0.74 | 0.8 |
| alarm | 0.62–1.20 | 1 |
| distress | 0.73–1.30 | 1 |
| growl | 0.48–0.68 | 0.75 |

The over-1 peaks were a six-legged species' square-wave buzz, so its voiced level is now ×0.7. The dominant frequencies came out near the fundamental or F1 (e.g. 290 Hz for a 136 Hz voice with F1 292 Hz), as a filtered harmonic source should.

**A panic in the game** (headless, the home system's Haikrai I, a herd of six Hon crawlers, ship flown at them): the mean distance from the ship went 48 → 13.6 (alert 0.82) → 8.9 (all startled) → 11.5, 16.1, 21.2, 26.4, 31.7, 36.7 units at one-second intervals, then they stood watching ~40 units off. Flown back over them, they bolted again. Once the ship had gone, the panic ended and was forgotten. 27 calls played meanwhile, alarms among them.

## Game mapping

**Panic** (`gen/panic.ts` `panicParams`):

- A herd bolts when the ship comes within `fleeRadius` = 18 units of one of its animals (3D distance from about the edge of its body). It looks up (no grazing) out to `alertFactor` = 2 flee radii, the alert distance being "by definition greater". Real flight distances would empty the planet in front of a UFO (sheep fled helicopters within 3 km), so 18 units is a few UFO widths: low over a herd scares it, passing high doesn't (the lowest altitude is 3).
- Each animal freezes for 0.1–0.6 s (stylised reaction time), then gallops at Fr = 2.5 (the middle of Alexander's 2–3: v = √(2.5·g·h), so it's slower on light worlds and for short legs) for `fleeFactor` = 1.6 flee radii (±15% each, scattered ±0.35 rad). It stops, turns round to face the threat and watches it as long as it stays within the alert distance (`hold`), as Kilgour's bulls kept their distance. Followed, it bolts again once it has stopped. Then, `calm` = 4 s after the threat has gone, it walks back at its walking speed. The panic is an offset laid over the herd's pure clock-driven walk, itself a pure function of the time since the startle, so it ends exactly where the clock has the herd and nothing is left of it.
- An animal taken by the beam or hit by the laser cries out (distress), and its herd bolts from where it was.
- The radar applies a herd's panic too, so it finds animals where they fled to.

**Voices** (`gen/animalVoice.ts`):

- Mass from the hip height (`animalMass`, Kilbourne & Hoffman via docs/research/animals.md). Pitch = 1000·M^−0.4 Hz × a species' own 0.75–1.35 (Fletcher's scatter), kept within 70–1600 Hz (stylised: a laptop speaker can't carry an elephant's 20 Hz; the formants and harmonics still tell the size).
- Tract L = 3.15 + 11.53·log₁₀M cm (±10%); formants (2n − (m + 1))·c/4L with c = 350 m/s. The mouth's closure m moves through each syllable (shut → open → shut), opened as wide as the species' mouth part allows (the creature editor's mouth size and spread). Without a mouth it hums nearly shut. Formants don't go below 120 Hz.
- A syllable lasts 0.15 of the longest utterance, 0.15 × 1.56·M^0.26 s, capped at 1.2 s (stylised).
- Six-legged species stridulate: a square wave shaken by 25–60 Hz pulses.
- Each kind of call follows Morton: contact and answer calls at the voice's own pitch; a grunt (grazing) at 0.75×, short and nearly shut; an alert snort at 1.1×, mostly breath; an alarm at 1.6×, tonal, 2–4 quick syllables; a distress cry at 1.9×, long and rough; a hunter's growl at 0.55×, very rough; a scream at 2.2×, as rough as the synthesiser goes, its rasp at a rate drawn log-evenly from Arnal's 30–150 Hz. The multipliers are stylised.
- **Screams** (`cargo/CargoBeam.ts`): an animal grabbed by the beam cries out (distress), then screams again and again all the way up, 0.08–0.3 s between screams, each one higher the higher it's carried (up to +35%, stylised), and screams as it falls if let go. The deep rasp takes about a third of a scream's power, so a scream is made 1.25× as loud (measured offline: peaks 0.46–1.15 across 8 species, beside the distress cry's 0.73–0.92).

**Talk** (`gen/animalTalk.ts`, stylised timings): per herd, slots of 4·M^0.13 s (half the breathing period's exponent, so giants still talk), 60% holding a conversation. One animal calls and up to 3 others answer in turn, each 0.15–0.7 s after the last call ended. Hunters open with a growl. Grazing animals grunt (30% per 5 s slot). In a panic, the animal nearest the threat gives the alarm and up to 3 others take it up over 2.5 s. All of it is hashed from the herd's seed and the slot, so the same moment holds the same exchange.

**Hearing** (`surface/AnimalSounds.ts`): power falls as 1/(1 + (d/r)²), with r = 22·(M/100 kg)^0.3 units. That is half Fletcher's M^0.6 range scaling, so a small animal is still heard from the ship (stylised). Calls are panned by the side they're on, and at most 8 play at once (alarms and distress always play). Air absorption is left out: at 0.5 dB/100 m it is under 1 dB across anything audible here.

## Open questions

- The tract-length fit (Moore & Mitchinson's eq. 7, citing their ref. [11]) gives rhesus monkeys 12–14 cm against Ghazanfar's 5.5–10 cm, and people 24 cm against ~17 cm (the 17 cm is from memory, unverified). It may be fitted to non-primates, or count the tract differently. The game's formants may sit ~30% low for their size; the trend is right.
- The red deer numbers come from a search summary of Reby & McComb, not the paper itself.
- Flight distance against body size: Blumstein 2006 (birds: larger flee sooner) and Blumstein 2015 (212 species: larger more tolerant) pull opposite ways, so the game uses one distance for all sizes.
- Atmospheres: formants scale with the speed of sound in the air, so a helium-rich world's animals would sound higher. Not done; every world uses 350 m/s.
